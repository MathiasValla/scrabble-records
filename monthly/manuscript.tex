\begin{abstract}
How many points can one Scrabble move score if it adds exactly $k$ tiles? We
answer this question for $k=1,\ldots,7$ under NWL23, the North American
tournament dictionary, and CSW24, the international Collins dictionary. The
answers range from 213 points for one tile to 1787 for seven. The proof
separates three questions: can the chosen words be scored together, can the
surrounding board be legal, and can that board arise in an actual game?
Elementary bounds reduce the first question to finite enumeration. Completion
arguments settle the few remaining boards, and explicit move histories settle
the third. The larger dictionary usually gives the larger record, but not for
six tiles.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Scrabble, extremal combinatorics,
computer-assisted proof, dictionary comparison.

\section{A two-tile surprise}

The play that prompted this article looks almost modest. We label the columns
A--O from left to right and the rows 1--15 from top to bottom. A still from the
round-17 broadcast of the 2026 Word Cup shows Evan Chester facing Steve Grob,
both listed in the event's top division \cite{wordcup}. The letters \word{AG}
were already side by side near the right edge. Chester put \word{Z} on L8 and
\word{S} on O8, around the old letters on M8 and N8, making \word{ZAGS}. The
\word{Z} landed on a double-letter square. The \word{S} landed on a triple-word
square and also completed the vertical word \word{VIDIOTS}. Those two little
tiles scored as follows. A \word{Z} is worth 10 points, doubled here to 20;
\word{A}, \word{G}, and \word{S} are worth 1, 2, and 1.
\[
  3(20+1+2+1)+3(4+1+2+1+1+1+1)=72+33=105
\]
points. The broadcast called it a competition record for a two-tile play. That
historical claim is only our starting point; none of the proofs below depends
on it.

We recall the rules we need. The board is a $15\times15$ grid, and H8 is its centre.
The bag contains 98 letter tiles of fixed multiplicities and two blanks, and a
player holds at most seven tiles. The first move must cover H8. Later, a player
may pass or place one to seven tiles in a single row or column, possibly with
old tiles between them; every square from the first tile to the last must then
be occupied, forming one contiguous main word. The occupied board must remain connected. Every
maximal horizontal or vertical string of length at least two that the move
creates must belong to the chosen dictionary \cite{rules}.

Each letter has a face value. Some squares double or triple the value of a new
tile; others double or triple an entire word containing that tile. A premium
works only when its square is first covered. All words made by the move are
scored: the word along the direction of play is the \emph{main word}, and the
perpendicular words through new tiles are \emph{crosswords}. Thus \word{ZAGS}
was the main word and \word{VIDIOTS} a crossword. A blank may represent any
letter but always scores zero. Playing all seven rack tiles adds a 50-point
bonus. The full tile list and premium-square coordinates are recorded in the
Supplementary Material.

This makes Chester's 105 points less mysterious, but it raises a cleaner
question. Fix $k$, the number of tiles placed on the turn. How large can the
score be if the players and the draws cooperate perfectly? Write this maximum
as $M_k(D)$, where $D$ is the permitted dictionary. We use NWL23, the North
American tournament dictionary, and CSW24, the international Collins
dictionary \cite{nwl,csw,wespa-open}. The dictionary is part of the rules: a
word accepted in one form of the game may fail in the other.

Real tournament records provide useful perspective. NASPA lists Karl
Khoshnaw's 392-point \word{CAZIQUES}, played in Manchester in 1982, and three
311-point plays in its North American tournament category \cite{naspa-records}.
Constructed positions go much higher. Scrabulizer records 1780- and 1785-point
constructions from 2008, and the contributor DejMar reported a 1787-point CSW15
construction in 2016 \cite{scrabulizer,dejmar}. In 2024 Bob Lucassen found the
1786-point NWL23 construction used below \cite{woogles}. Our concern is not
priority for those numbers, but proof: are they really the best possible for
their stated dictionaries, and what happens for every smaller value of $k$?

\begin{theorem}[The fourteen records]\label{thm:records}
For ordinary move scoring on the standard English board,
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrr}
k&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\\hline
M_k(\mathrm{NWL23})&213&459&882&1130&1401&1723&1786\\
M_k(\mathrm{CSW24})&225&483&912&1349&1478&1721&1787
\end{array}
\]
Every value is attained in a legal game.
\end{theorem}

We exclude challenge bonuses, time penalties, endgame rack transfers, and
unchallenged invalid words. The theorem is existential: the positions need not
be sensible tournament strategy. The two finite word sets are versioned by
counts and SHA-256 hashes in the Supplementary Material, making the statement
independent of later dictionary revisions.

\section{What an upper bound must remember}

A candidate record has three layers. First come the words scored on the final
turn. They must share letters without asking for more tiles than the bag
contains. Second comes the rest of the board: it must be connected and contain
no stray nonwords. Third comes time: the board must be constructible from an
opening word through legal turns. For an upper bound we may safely forget some
of these restrictions; for equality we must restore all of them.

Suppose first that $k\ge2$. By reflecting across the main diagonal, we may take
the move to be horizontal. It makes one horizontal main word $W$ and at most
$k$ vertical crosswords, one through each new tile. A one-tile move makes at
most two words and is treated in both orientations.

\begin{lemma}[Deletion fragments]\label{lem:deletion}
Delete the newly placed tiles from a word scored on the final turn. Every
remaining consecutive fragment of length at least two was already a word.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The letters of such a fragment were already present. An internal fragment ends
where the board was empty before the move. An exterior fragment cannot extend
beyond the final word, which was chosen maximal. Thus every nontrivial fragment
was a maximal string before the turn. A one-letter or empty fragment imposes no
condition: the single old tile may have been connected in the other direction.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Premium ceiling]\label{lem:premium}
The main-word multiplier of a $k$-tile move is at most
$3^{\min(k,3)}$. A crossword multiplier is at most three.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
An edge row contains three triple-word squares and no double-word squares. The
middle row contains two triple-word squares and one double-word square, whose
product is only 18. Every other row contains at most two double-word squares and
no triple-word square. At most $k$ of these premiums can be activated.
Transposition gives the same statement for columns. A crossword contains only
one new square, so it activates at most one word premium.
\end{proof}

This bound concerns multipliers, not the whole score. To keep the remaining
arithmetic exact, let $v(a)$ be the face value of the letter $a$, and let
$\lambda_p$ and $\mu_p$ be the letter and word multipliers at square $p$. If
$I$ indexes the new letters of $W$, then the all-real-tile score of the main
word is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 A(W,I)=\left(\prod_{i\in I}\mu_{p_i}\right)
 \left[\sum_{i=1}^{|W|}v(W_i)+
       \sum_{i\in I}(\lambda_{p_i}-1)v(W_i)\right].
\end{equation}
Old premium squares do not appear: they never reactivate.

For a fitted main word $P$, let $C_j(P)$ be the largest all-real score of any
deletion-valid crossword through its $j$th new tile, including the option of no
crossword. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:independent}
 U(P)=A(P)+\sum_{j=1}^k C_j(P)+50\mathbf1_{\{k=7\}}
\end{equation}
is an upper bound. It gives each cross its favorite surrounding letters
independently, even when those choices compete for the same physical tiles.
That deliberate generosity makes $U(P)$ useful for discarding most placements.

The candidate patterns must also respect the bag. A crossing square is one physical tile even
though it contributes to two words. For each scored square $s$, add its
coefficients in all scored words and call the sum $c_s$. If the board displays
$n_a$ copies of $a$ but the bag has only $B_a$ real copies, at least
$e_a=(n_a-B_a)_+$ of them are blanks. Order the coefficients of the $a$-squares
as $c_{a,1}\le\cdots\le c_{a,n_a}$.

\begin{lemma}[Exact blank penalty]\label{lem:blank}
The scored skeleton is impossible if $\sum_a e_a>2$. Otherwise its greatest
score on the scored squares under the tile supply is its all-real score minus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:blank}
 \sum_a v(a)\sum_{j=1}^{e_a}c_{a,j}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A blank on an $a$-square $s$ loses exactly $v(a)c_s$. If a blank occupies a
larger-coefficient square while a real $a$ occupies a smaller one, swapping the
two cannot lower the score. Repeating the swap places the forced blanks on the
least expensive squares. The real-tile supplies are independent, and the two
blanks are their only shared resource.
\end{proof}

For the largest cases, a second upper bound keeps the Cartesian products small.
Give each letter $a$ any nonnegative rational price $p_a$. For an option $o$ in
crossword slot $j$, let $s_{jo}$ be its score and $a_{jo}$ its vector of old
tile counts, excluding the shared new square. Let $m$ count each physical
main-word square once, let $B$ be the real bag, and put
\[
 z_j=\max_o(s_{jo}-p\cdot a_{jo}),\qquad
 t=\max\bigl(0,\max_a(p_a-v(a))\bigr).
\]

\begin{lemma}[Resource prices]\label{lem:prices}
Every product through that main placement has pre-bingo score at most
\[
 A+p\cdot(B-m)+\sum_jz_j+2t.
\]
For any options already chosen, their nonnegative reduced costs
$z_j-s_{jo}+p\cdot a_{jo}$ may be subtracted from this bound.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $e_a$ blanks represent letter $a$, feasibility gives
$m+\sum_j a_{jo_j}\le B+e$ coordinatewise and $\sum_a e_a\le2$. Each blank
loses at least $v(a)$. Hence the score is at most
\[
 A+\sum_j(s_{jo_j}-p\cdot a_{jo_j})+p\cdot(B-m)
   +\sum_a(p_a-v(a))e_a.
\]
The last sum is at most $2t$, and every unchosen option is bounded by $z_j$.
\end{proof}

The prices used in the certificates are multiples of $1/1024$, so every
comparison is an integer comparison. How the prices were found is irrelevant
to validity: any nonnegative prices satisfying the displayed inequalities give
the bound.

We now make the finite reduction precise. A \emph{scoring skeleton} is a triple
$(N,F,E)$. Here $N$ is the set of new squares, $F$ assigns letters to the
union of the main word and its crosswords, and $E$ consists of squares forced
empty: the squares just beyond those maximal words, and both perpendicular
neighbors of a new tile that makes no crossword. Shared squares occur only once
in $F$. A \emph{completion} extends $F$ to a final board, leaves $E$ empty,
uses the common 100-tile supply with at most two blanks, is connected, and has
every maximal string of length at least two in $D$. If the skeleton contains
old tiles, its move is not the opening move, so its completion must also contain
H8. Every legal move has a completion; the converse need not hold.

The Chester play gives a small example. Its new set is
$N=\{\mathrm{L8},\mathrm{O8}\}$. The map $F$ spells \word{ZAGS} on L8--O8
and \word{VIDIOTS} on O2--O8, counting O8 only once. The forced-empty set
contains K8, O1, O9, L7, and L9: these prevent either scored word from
extending and prevent the new \word{Z} from making an unlisted crossword.
Deleting the new letters leaves the old words \word{AG} and \word{VIDIOT}.
This skeleton remembers exactly what the final score forces and nothing else.

\begin{lemma}[Line relaxation]\label{lem:line}
For one row, write a forced letter as itself, a forced empty square as
\word{\#}, and every other square as a dot. A left-to-right recursion decides
exactly whether the dots can be filled or left empty so that each maximal
occupied interval is either one square or a word in $D$. Every completion of a
scoring skeleton passes this test in every row and column.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
At the first unprocessed square, either leave an optional dot empty, or choose
each dictionary interval beginning there that matches all forced letters and
avoids every \word{\#}; continue after a separating empty square. These are
exactly the possibilities for the first maximal interval. Induction on the
unprocessed suffix proves the first statement. A true completion supplies one
such filling. Allowing isolated squares and allowing different lines to reuse
the same remaining tiles only enlarge the set of completions.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Hooks and reduced boards]\label{lem:hooks}
Suppose an old top-edge letter or block has only new or forced-empty horizontal
neighbors. Every completion contains a perpendicular dictionary word through
at least one of its squares. After one such \emph{mandatory hook} is chosen for
each block, every completion contains a reduced board obtained by retaining
only the dictionary intervals needed to repair its forced nonwords. If no such
reduced board respects one shared tile supply, no completion exists.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Before the final move, the block cannot connect through the new squares or
through the outside of the board. Since the old board was connected, it must
connect downward through a maximal perpendicular word. For each forced illegal
run in a row, a completion has a unique maximal horizontal dictionary word
containing that run; retain its squares and discard every optional square not
so selected. This is one of the enumerated reduced boards and is a subboard of
the completion. Finally, every retained square is one physical tile. Enumerating
all hook and repair choices while debiting their union from a single multiset
therefore rejects only tile-infeasible boards.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Completeness of the finite reduction]
\label{prop:complete}
Fix $D$, $k$, and a pre-bingo threshold $q$. The following enumeration contains
the scoring skeleton of every legal $k$-tile move scoring at least $q$ before
the seven-tile bonus.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose a word $W\in D$, the $k$ new positions in $W$, an orientation,
and a fit on the board; retain only deletion-valid choices.
\item Retain a fit if its independent bound \eqref{eq:independent}, without the
bonus, is at least $q$. The resource-price bound may reject it only when the
integer inequality in Lemma~\ref{lem:prices} is below $q$.
\item Enumerate the Cartesian product of all deletion-valid crossword options,
merge shared squares, and apply the exact blank penalty.
\item Apply centre occupancy, line compatibility, mandatory-hook, reduced-board,
and shared-bag tests as needed.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, if every retained skeleton fails one of these necessary tests,
no such move exists.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take a legal move at or above $q$. Reflection lets us make its main word
horizontal when $k\ge2$. Its actual main word, new positions, orientation, and
location occur in step 1 by Lemma~\ref{lem:deletion}. Its actual crosswords are
among the independently chosen maxima, so it cannot fail step 2. They occur as
one tuple in step 3; merging squares gives its physical multiset, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:blank} gives the best possible placement of any forced blanks.
Its final board is a completion of the resulting skeleton. Hence it passes the
centre and compatible-line conditions, contains one of the enumerated words
for each mandatory hook, contains the corresponding reduced board, and obeys
the shared bag. Thus no stated rejection can remove the chosen move.
\end{proof}

Finally, an arrangement of valid words is not yet a game. The small boards use
the following simple observation; the nearly full seven-tile boards receive
explicit two-player rack certificates.

\begin{lemma}[Sparse histories]\label{lem:racks}
A legal positive-scoring placement history using at most 86 physical tiles can
be realized by two players with legal racks and draws.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Reserve for the second player seven tiles unused by the history, and let that
player pass. Order the first player's required tiles by move, append seven
unused tiles, deal the first seven, and draw along this list. Before each move
the rack contains every tile that move requires. At most $86+7=93$ stream tiles
are needed after the opponent's rack is reserved. Because every placement
scores, the intervening single passes do not end the game; the appended filler
tiles also prevent the builder from emptying a rack before the record move.
Any tiles not yet named are placed at the inaccessible end of the bag.
\end{proof}


\section{One and two tiles}

For a square $p$ and a letter $a$, let $H(p,a)$ be the set of all
deletion-valid occurrences of dictionary words that fit horizontally through
$p$ with distinguished letter $a$ at $p$. Define $V(p,a)$ vertically. These
sets are finite and explicit.

\begin{proposition}
$M_1(\mathrm{NWL23})=213$ and $M_1(\mathrm{CSW24})=225$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A one-tile move makes at most one horizontal and one vertical word. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:deletion}, it therefore appears in
\[
 \bigcup_{p,a}\bigl(H(p,a)\cup V(p,a)\cup
                       (H(p,a)\times V(p,a))\bigr).
\]
For every member of this union, the enumeration applies the premium at $p$,
counts the common new square once in the tile supply but once in each word's
score, and applies Lemma~\ref{lem:blank}. This gives maxima 213 and 225.

For completeness, the implementation sorts each $H(p,a)$ and $V(p,a)$ by its
all-real contribution. During the nested scan, the first unvisited term bounds
every later term. A suffix is skipped only when that bound is smaller than the
incumbent; induction over the sorted lists shows that no skipped pair can
improve it. This branch-and-bound changes the running time, not the set being
maximized.

Equality is simple. In NWL23, adding \word{S} at O15 completes
\word{HYDROXYZINES} and \word{MICROTECHNIQUES}; both meet the new triple-word
square, giving $3(38+33)=213$. In CSW24, the same geometry completes
\word{METHOXYBENZENES} and \word{QUICKSILVERINGS}, giving
$3(41+34)=225$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
$M_2(\mathrm{NWL23})=459$ and $M_2(\mathrm{CSW24})=483$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} with $q=459$. The two $k=2$ rows of
Table~\ref{tab:audit} show that the largest exact scoring products are 459 and
483. These are upper bounds even though the product stage ignores connectivity
and chronology.

For equality in NWL23, play \word{Y} at H15 and \word{S} at O15 to make
\word{PAYCHEQUES}. The main word covers two triple-word squares; the crosses are
\word{XYLOGRAPHY} and \word{KATZENJAMMERS}. Thus
\[
 9\cdot29+3\cdot29+3\cdot37=459.
\]
In CSW24, \word{JAYHAWKERS}, \word{QUALIFICATORY}, and
\word{METHOXYBENZENES} give
\[
 9\cdot30+3\cdot30+3\cdot41=483.
\]
The Supplementary Material gives legal histories for all four boards.
\end{proof}

\section{Three tiles}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:three}
$M_3(\mathrm{NWL23})=882$ and $M_3(\mathrm{CSW24})=912$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} and Table~\ref{tab:audit} find no NWL23 scoring
product at 883 or above, while the largest CSW24 product at that threshold is
912. Hence the two upper bounds are 882 and 912.

Both records use the main word \word{WHIPPERSNAPPERS}, made by placing
\word{W,S,S} at A15, H15, O15. Four displayed \word{P}'s force two blanks, so
the tripled main word scores $27(29-6)=621$. In NWL23 the crosses
\word{RICKSHAW}, \word{OBJECTIVIZES}, and \word{DEMISEMIQUAVERS} add
$60+105+96$, for 882. In CSW24 the crosses \word{KABELJOUW},
\word{METHOXYBENZENES}, and \word{QUALIFICATIVES} add $75+123+93$, for
912. The supplementary histories use 61 and 65 tiles, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:racks} supplies legal racks and draws.
\end{proof}

Here the entire thirty-point difference comes from the crosswords. The final
\word{W} of \word{KABELJOUW}, together with the short supporting words
\word{EE} and \word{OU}, is available only in Collins.

\section{Four tiles}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:four}
$M_4(\mathrm{NWL23})=1130$ and $M_4(\mathrm{CSW24})=1349$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} and Table~\ref{tab:audit} exclude NWL23 scores
of 1131 or more and CSW24 scores of 1350 or more.

For NWL23, place \word{O,Y,O,S} at A15, C15, H15, O15 to make
\word{OXYHAEMOGLOBINS}. The main word scores 891. The crosses
\word{BALDACHINO}, \word{PLETHYSMOGRAPHY}, \word{JOCKO}, and
\word{VENTRILOQUIZES} add $54+26+54+105$, giving 1130. Two old \word{H}'s in
the column-C cross are blanks.

For CSW24, place \word{M,B,Z,S} at A15, H15, K15, O15 to make
\word{METHOXYBENZENES}. It scores $27\cdot41=1107$. The crosses
\word{HYPOCHONDRIASM}, \word{JAMB}, \word{WOOTZ}, and
\word{QUICKSILVERINGS} add $78+45+17+102$, giving 1349. An old blank
\word{H} accounts for the reduction from 90 to 78 in the first cross. The two
histories use 84 and 72 tiles and therefore satisfy Lemma~\ref{lem:racks}.
\end{proof}

One product tied at 1349 reveals why reachability cannot be ignored. It contains
\word{PROVERB} from H9 to H15. Since the scored word is maximal, H8 must be
empty; but the opening move covers H8, and tiles are never removed. The scoring
pattern is sound, yet no game can reach it.

\section{Five tiles}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:five}
$M_5(\mathrm{NWL23})=1401$ and $M_5(\mathrm{CSW24})=1478$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} excludes NWL23 scores of 1402 or more. For
CSW24, the ten scoring skeletons at 1479 or more all contain maximal
\word{PROVERB} from H9 to H15. Maximality forces H8 to be empty, but every one
is a nonopening position and H8 has been occupied since the first move. Thus
they are all impossible.

The NWL23 construction places \word{D,L,O,Z,E} at A15, E15, H15, L15, O15 to
make \word{DICHLOROBENZENE}. The main word scores $27(32+10)=1134$. Its five
crosses add
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 &\word{HYPERVENTILATED}:81,\qquad \word{OVERFULFILL}:20,\\
 &\word{JOCKO}:54,\qquad \word{QUARTZ}:34,\qquad
 \word{MYXAMOEBAE}:78.
 \end{aligned}
\]
for 1401.

The CSW24 construction places \word{M,H,B,Z,S} at A15, D15, H15, K15, O15 to
make \word{METHOXYBENZENES}. The new \word{H} is doubled, so the main word
scores $27(41+4)=1215$. Its five crosses contribute
$78+21+45+17+102=263$, giving 1478. The histories use 78 and 81 tiles.
\end{proof}


\section{Six tiles}

Six tiles are the first case in which plausible scoring patterns survive the
bag calculation and require a systematic completion argument.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:six}
$M_6(\mathrm{NWL23})=1723$ and $M_6(\mathrm{CSW24})=1721$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For NWL23, apply the compatible-row test to the twenty skeletons listed in
Table~\ref{tab:audit}. Four force the incompatible row
\[
 \word{CO.Q...D...G..A}.
\]
The other sixteen leave an old \word{X} at B1 with A1 and C1 unavailable
before the move. It must be joined downward by a mandatory hook. None of the
165 NWL23 words of board length beginning with \word{X} matches the forced
letters and leaves all rows compatible. Proposition~\ref{prop:complete} now
excludes 1724.

The attaining move places \word{O,X,P,B,Z,E} at A1, B1, D1, H1, L1, O1,
forming \word{OXYPHENBUTAZONE}. The main word scores 1458, and its six crosses
add $69+11+34+57+31+63=265$, for 1723. Its 22 setup moves place 79 tiles;
the six-tile record move brings the board total
to 85, so Lemma~\ref{lem:racks} applies.

For CSW24, compatible rows and mandatory hooks leave seven skeletons. For each,
the shared-bag completion enumerates every hook and every required row interval
jointly, debiting their union from one physical tile multiset. None survives,
so 1722 is impossible.

The CSW24 move places \word{O,Y,P,B,Z,E} at A1, C1, D1, H1, L1, O1. Again the
main word is \word{OXYPHENBUTAZONE} and scores 1458. Its crosses are
\word{OPACIFYING}, \word{YARAKS}, \word{PREQUALIFIED},
\word{BRAINWASHING}, \word{ZOOGAMETES}, and \word{EJACULATIONS}, worth
$63+13+29+63+32+63=263$. The 25 setup moves place 87 tiles, and the record
move brings the board total to 93. One
player reserves the seven tiles of the last setup move, \word{PACIFYING}; the
full rack-and-draw certificate verifies that neither player goes out early.
\end{proof}

The international dictionary has far more playable entries, but its six-tile
maximum is two points lower. The NWL23 construction relies on
\word{OPACIFICATIONS}, which is absent from CSW24. Dictionaries are not nested,
so their sizes do not order their records.

\section{Seven tiles}

For seven tiles, the 50-point bonus is constant. We therefore state exclusion
thresholds before the bonus.

\begin{lemma}[Three-component connector]\label{lem:connector}
If at most $R$ new squares connect three old components, some connecting subset
is the union of two simple paths from one component to the other two; neither
path passes through the third component.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Contract the old components to three terminals. Choose an inclusion-minimal
connecting set of new vertices, take a spanning tree, and prune nonterminal
leaves. Either one terminal lies on the path between the other two, or the tree
has one branching vertex with three terminal branches. In the first case root
at the middle terminal; in the second root at any terminal. Two root-to-terminal
paths cover the tree, so their union uses no more than the original $R$ new
vertices. If a lifted path meets its target component early, stop there.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:seven}
$M_7(\mathrm{NWL23})=1786$ and $M_7(\mathrm{CSW24})=1787$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{NWL23.} At pre-bingo score 1737, the compatible-row and mandatory-hook
tests leave the 34 skeletons shown in Table~\ref{tab:audit}. All use
\word{OXYPHENBUTAZONE} across the top row. Removing the seven new letters
leaves old top-row blocks that must connect downward: the board edge is above
them, while the intervening new squares were empty before the turn. Enumerating
every mandatory dictionary hook and every required row repair eliminates 33
skeletons. The last yields 193 reduced boards.

Each reduced board has three components, both blanks already committed, and at
most six real tiles left. By Lemma~\ref{lem:connector}, any completion contains
one of the enumerated two-path connector cores. All three choices of root, all
simple paths of the permitted length, and every multiset-respecting letter
assignment are tried. For each assignment, every row and then every column is
allowed to use the entire leftover multiset independently when repairing its
nonwords. This relaxation considers 24,293 cores and 5,518,820 assignments.
Only six pass every row, and each fails a column. Thus no pre-bingo score of
1737 is possible, so 1787 is impossible.

Lucassen's construction places \word{O,X,P,B,N,Z,E} on the seven empty squares
of the top-row \word{OXYPHENBUTAZONE}. Its words contribute
\[
1458+69+11+34+13+57+31+63+50=1786.
\]
The board uses 97 tiles. A two-player certificate reserves
\word{LADDERLIKE} for the second player's last setup move and follows both
racks and every draw through the 24 setup moves and the record move.

\emph{CSW24.} At pre-bingo score 1738, the first necessary tests leave nine
skeletons. For each, the shared-bag completion enumerates every mandatory hook
and row repair together and finds none. Proposition~\ref{prop:complete}
therefore excludes every total of 1788 or more.

For equality, place \word{O,X,P,B,A,Z,E} at A1, B1, D1, H1, K1, L1, O1. The
main word is \word{OXYPHENBUTAZONE}; the crosses are \word{OPACIFYING},
\word{XED}, \word{PREQUALIFIED}, \word{BRAINWASHING},
\word{AMELIORATIVE}, \word{ZOOGAMETES}, and \word{EJACULATIONS}. The score is
\[
1458+63+11+30+63+17+32+63+50=1787.
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:seven} shows the board. Its 27 setup moves place 89 tiles; the
record move brings the board total to 96. The
second player reserves the seven tiles of the last setup move,
\word{PACIFYING}, then draws four tiles and does not go out. The first player
holds \word{ABEOPXZ}, plays the record, and goes out. The certificate verifies
every intermediate word, blank, rack, draw, and score.
\end{proof}

Propositions~\ref{prop:three}--\ref{prop:seven}, together with the one- and

\section{Reproducibility}

The accompanying source package regenerates all fourteen finite enumerations
from the two identified digital dictionaries. Independent programs verify each
attaining history, including alignment, word membership, the centre-square
rule, incidental words, permanent blanks, physical tile counts, racks, and
draws. Small artificial dictionaries test the placement enumeration for every
$k=2,\ldots,7$; further tests target transposition, boundary fragments,
three-blank impossibility, shared-square penalties, and completion pruning.

The top-level driver is \word{scripts/reproduce\_release.py}. It compiles the
two enumeration programs, exports and hashes both word sets, repeats every
upper-bound stage in Table~\ref{tab:audit}, and then invokes separate forward
verifiers for the fourteen histories. The Supplementary Material gives exact
filenames and SHA-256 hashes for the one-tile and consolidated upper-bound
certificates, the witness collection, the fresh-run state and verdict, and the
dense NWL23 rack certificate.

The proof is computer-assisted, not formally verified in a proof assistant.
Its trust base consists of the stated reductions, integer arithmetic, the
published source, the compiler and interpreter, and the two version-matched
dictionaries. Licensed dictionary data are identified by cryptographic hashes
but are not redistributed. The Supplementary Material places each of the
fourteen record boards and its complete tile-placement history on one page;
passes and rack refills remain in the machine-readable certificates.

The point of the calculation is ultimately simple. A huge word and three
triple-word squares are only the beginning. The same physical blank may affect
two scores; an innocent-looking predecessor may belong to the wrong dictionary;
and the last bridge between two parts of a board may exhaust the bag. A record
needs all three layers at once: a collection of scoring words, a legal board,
and a game that can actually reach it.
